## Description

Participants turn responsible AI principles into controls that a team can operate and an auditor can check. Working on one fictional public-sector organisation with 30 AI systems in use or in development, they build an AI inventory, tier each system by risk, assign accountability with a responsibility matrix, design review gates for every lifecycle stage from intake to retirement, and draft an acceptable-use policy with prohibited categories. They finish with a governance starter pack that they defend before a mock governance committee.

## Learning outcomes

By the end, the participant can:

- Explain how principles, policies, standards, controls and evidence relate, and where each sits in an AI governance framework.
- Prepare an AI system inventory with the fields needed for ownership, purpose, data, model source, risk tier and lifecycle stage.
- Classify AI systems into four risk levels and record the factors that decided each level.
- Draft a responsibility matrix that assigns who is accountable, responsible, consulted and informed for each governance decision.
- Draft an acceptable-use policy with prohibited and restricted application categories, exception handling and a review date.
- Design lifecycle review gates from intake to retirement, with entry criteria, required evidence and the approving role for each risk level.

## Prerequisites

- Describes in plain words how a machine learning model and a language model assistant are built, tested and put into use
- Reads an organisational policy and identifies who must do what, and when

## Topics

### From principles to controls

- National AI ethics principles and their four risk levels
- Principles, policies, standards, procedures, controls and evidence
- International frameworks as support: OECD principles, NIST AI Risk Management Framework functions, AI management system structure
- What makes a control operable: owner, trigger, frequency, test

### Accountability and roles

- Developer, provider, deployer and operator responsibilities
- Governance committee, system owner, risk, legal, privacy and security roles
- Responsibility matrices and decision rights
- Escalation paths
- Three lines of defence applied to AI

### The AI inventory

- What counts as an AI system, including features inside purchased software
- Inventory fields and ownership
- Finding undeclared AI use through procurement, expense and network records
- Keeping the inventory current

### Risk tiering

- Four-level categorisation from little or no risk to unacceptable
- Tiering factors: impact on people, autonomy, scale, reversibility, data sensitivity
- Proportionality: what each level triggers

### Lifecycle gates

- Intake and use-case screening
- Design and data approval
- Evaluation and deployment approval
- Monitoring, change and re-approval
- Retirement as a gate (retirement planning in depth is LDS-AIG-203)

### Policy set and documentation

- Acceptable-use policy and prohibited categories
- Staff rules for generative AI tools, coding agents and staff-built automations
- Data classification and residency rules for external model services
- Documentation minimums per risk level (full impact assessment is in LDS-AIG-303)
- Records and retention

### Making governance work

- Governance as a service to delivery teams
- Governance as an enabler in the national AI adoption framework
- Entity-level evidence: national AI ethics badges and accreditation of AI service providers
- Measures: inventory coverage, gate cycle time, open exceptions
- Signs of governance in name only
