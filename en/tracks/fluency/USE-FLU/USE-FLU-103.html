USE-FLU-103 How AI Systems Learn and Where They Fail

  • Level 100 · Explain
  • Primer
  • 6 h
  • Audience: beginner

Track

Data & AI Fluency

Stream

USE-FLU Fluency for Everyone

Level

Level 100 · Explain

Audience

beginner

Format

Primer: one facilitated day of six sessions or six self-paced modules, with a scenario test

Total hours

6 h

Prerequisites

  • Basic arithmetic with percentages; no programming or statistics

Description

A non-technical primer on how learned systems are made and why they fail. Participants trace the path from data to model to decision for a workplace tool, train a small classifier without code and watch it break on shifted data, work out why a high detection rate and a high accuracy figure both mislead when the event is rare, look for leakage and bias in dataset descriptions, and collect fluent but wrong answers from a language model. The course ends with a written critique of a vendor's accuracy claim that names the evidence still missing.

Learning outcomes

By the end, the participant can:

  1. Awareness

    Describe the path from data to model to decision for a workplace AI system and mark where people make choices.

    Given a synthetic case of an automated leave-request triage tool.

    Evidence a labelled pipeline diagram showing data sources, training, testing, threshold and human review.

  2. Awareness

    Explain why a fluent language model answer can be wrong, including next-word prediction, missing or outdated knowledge and the tendency to agree with the user.

    Given four model answers with known errors.

    Evidence a one-line cause per error that matches the reference on at least three of four.

  3. Applied

    Compute the false alarms, misses and share of real flags that stated detection rates imply when the event is rare, and show why overall accuracy misleads.

    Given a fraud detector said to catch 95% of fraud and clear 95% of honest cases, for fraud in one case in a hundred, and a rule that flags nothing.

    Evidence a completed table for 1,000 cases for both, with the share of flags that are real and the accuracy of the flag-nothing rule.

  4. Awareness

    Describe how overfitting and data shift lower a model's performance on new data.

    Given a no-code classifier trained in class, its test results and a later batch of shifted synthetic data.

    Evidence both accuracy figures recorded with a two-line explanation of the drop.

  5. Applied

    Analyse a dataset description for leakage, unrepresentative coverage and unreliable labels, and state who could be harmed by each problem.

    Given three dataset cards for synthetic hiring, clinic appointment and loan cases.

    Evidence an issue list per card that names at least two of the three planted problems.

  6. Applied

    Draft a critique of a vendor accuracy claim that asks for the missing evidence: test data, base rate, error types, subgroup results and conditions of use.

    Given a one-page vendor brochure for a CV-screening tool.

    Evidence a half-page critique scored on a four-criterion rubric.

  7. Awareness

    Distinguish a model from the system around it, including rules, retrieval, tools and human review, and identify where a human must stay in the loop.

    Given three short system descriptions.

    Evidence scenario items on system boundaries and oversight answered correctly.

Topics

From data to decision

  • Task types: classify, forecast, recommend, generate
  • Features, labels and examples
  • Model versus system: rules, retrieval, tools, human review
  • Who chooses what in the pipeline

Learning and generalising

  • Training and testing
  • Overfitting
  • Data shift and stale models
  • Why more data is not always better data

Errors, base rates and thresholds

  • False positives and false negatives
  • Base rates and rare events
  • Why accuracy alone misleads
  • Thresholds as policy choices

Leakage and bias

  • Leakage: when the answer hides in the data
  • Representation, label and proxy bias
  • Historical patterns repeated
  • Results by subgroup

Language models: fluent is not correct

  • Next-word prediction
  • Knowledge cut-off and missing local knowledge
  • Invented sources and figures
  • Agreeing with the user

Questioning a claim

  • What was tested, on whom, under which conditions
  • Error types and who bears their cost
  • Further evidence to ask for
  • Automation bias: people deferring to the system
  • Human oversight and the right to challenge

Tools and platforms

These are examples. Any equivalent tool may be used instead.

No-code model builder
Orange Data Mining, a browser-based image or sound classifier trainer
Spreadsheet for base-rate tables
LibreOffice Calc, Microsoft Excel, Google Sheets
Language model chat
a locally served small open-weight model, any approved commercial assistant

How the work is assessed

  • Scenario test of 15 items, AI-off (50%)
  • Marked lab outputs: pipeline map, base-rate table, dataset review (20%)
  • Written critique of an accuracy claim scored on a four-criterion rubric (30%)
To pass
70% overall and at least 'meets' on every criterion of the critique rubric

Use of AI in the course

AI-off for the scenario test, the base-rate table and the first draft of the critique. AI-full in the fluent-errors lab, where the model is the object of study. AI-tutor allowed for explaining terms during the self-paced modules.

AI-off: no AI assistance. AI-tutor: an assistant that hints and explains but does not give the answer. AI-full: any assistance; the participant owns the result and defends it.
