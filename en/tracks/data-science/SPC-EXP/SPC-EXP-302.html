SPC-EXP-302 Causal Inference from Observational Data

  • Level 300 · Develop
  • Intensive
  • 36 h
  • Audience: practitioner

Track

Data Science

Stream

SPC-EXP Experimentation and Causal Inference

Level

Level 300 · Develop

Audience

practitioner

Format

Intensive: 4 teaching days and a capstone day, with guided pre-work and a tracked follow-up task

Total hours

36 h

Prerequisites

  • SPC-STA-301 Regression and Generalised Linear Modelling
  • Designs and reads a randomised experiment at the depth of SPC-EXP-201
  • Writes analysis notebooks in Python or R

Description

Participants answer causal questions where no experiment was run. They write each question as an estimand, draw a causal graph and derive what must be adjusted for, then estimate effects with matching and weighting, difference-in-differences with staggered adoption, synthetic control, regression discontinuity and instrumental variables. Each method is practised on a public or synthetic dataset where the answer is known or has been debated in the literature. Every analysis ends with a sensitivity analysis and a plain statement of the assumptions a reader must accept. The capstone is a full observational analysis defended before a panel.

Learning outcomes

By the end, the participant can:

  1. Applied

    Draft a causal graph (directed acyclic graph, DAG) for a stated question and derive a valid adjustment set, identifying colliders and bad controls.

    Given three case descriptions with candidate variables.

    Evidence graphs and adjustment sets that match the reference, with each excluded variable justified.

  2. Applied

    Apply propensity-score matching and inverse probability weighting to estimate an average treatment effect, checking overlap and covariate balance.

    Given the public job-training data used in the classic comparison of experimental and observational estimates.

    Evidence a notebook with balance tables, overlap plots and estimates compared with the experimental benchmark.

  3. Applied

    Apply difference-in-differences to estimate a programme effect, including an event-study plot and an estimator suited to staggered adoption.

    Given a synthetic panel of regions adopting a programme in different years, with a known effect that changes over time.

    Evidence two-way fixed-effects and staggered-adoption estimates with a written explanation of why they differ.

  4. Applied

    Implement synthetic control, regression discontinuity and instrumental-variable estimates, each with its standard diagnostic.

    Given one public case per method: a regional tobacco policy, an election threshold and a lottery-based offer.

    Evidence three short analyses with placebo tests, bandwidth and manipulation checks, and first-stage strength.

  5. Performance

    Defend the choice of an identification strategy for a new causal question against at least two alternatives.

    Given a policy question, a data inventory and limits on time and data access.

    Evidence a design memo naming the estimand, the strategy, its key assumption and the falsification tests planned.

  6. Performance

    Evaluate how sensitive an estimate is to unmeasured confounding and state how strong a confounder would have to be to change the conclusion.

    Given an observational estimate from the participant's own capstone analysis.

    Evidence a sensitivity section with bounds and a plain-language interpretation.

  7. Performance

    Deliver an end-to-end observational analysis from question to defended conclusion.

    Given a public or synthetic dataset and a question chosen from a list.

    Evidence a repository and four-page report defended before a panel that challenges one assumption.

Topics

Questions, estimands and potential outcomes

  • Average, on-the-treated and local effects
  • Target populations
  • Why prediction is not causation
  • Pre-analysis plans for observational work

Causal graphs and identification

  • Directed acyclic graphs and the back-door criterion
  • Colliders, mediators and bad controls
  • The front-door idea
  • What a graph cannot tell you

Matching and weighting

  • Propensity scores
  • Matching and inverse probability weighting
  • Estimators that combine outcome and propensity models
  • Overlap, trimming and balance diagnostics

Panel designs

  • Difference-in-differences and parallel trends
  • Event studies
  • Staggered adoption and effects that change over time
  • Interrupted time series and its limits
  • Synthetic control and placebo tests

Discontinuities and instruments

  • Sharp and fuzzy regression discontinuity
  • Bandwidth choice and manipulation tests
  • Instrument relevance and exclusion
  • Weak instruments and the local average treatment effect

Sensitivity and partial identification

  • Sensitivity to unmeasured confounding
  • Bounds and partial identification
  • Falsification and placebo outcomes

Reporting causal claims

  • Assumptions in plain words
  • Checking assistant-written analysis code
  • Limitations sections that a decision maker can use

Tools and platforms

These are examples. Any equivalent tool may be used instead.

Analysis language and notebooks
Python with Jupyter or marimo, R with Quarto
Causal graph drawing and identification
DAGitty, DoWhy
Regression and panel estimation
statsmodels, linearmodels, fixest for R
Matching and weighting
MatchIt for R, DoWhy, scikit-learn for propensity models
Quasi-experimental estimators
did for R, Synth for R, a regression discontinuity package with bias-corrected intervals for R or Python, course-provided Python implementations
Sensitivity analysis
sensemakr, EValue for R

How the work is assessed

  • Supervised check without AI assistance on graphs and identification (20%)
  • Graded labs (30%)
  • Capstone analysis and report (40%)
  • Panel defence (10%)
To pass
70% overall, at least 60% on the capstone, and a pass on the identification question in the defence

Use of AI in the course

AI-off for causal graphs and identification in the supervised check and the first lab. AI-tutor in the method labs. AI-full for capstone code, with a verification note, while the design memo is defended live.

AI-off: no AI assistance. AI-tutor: an assistant that hints and explains but does not give the answer. AI-full: any assistance; the participant owns the result and defends it.
