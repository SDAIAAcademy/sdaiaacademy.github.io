Description

Participants build Bayesian models that support decisions and judge models built by others. On synthetic burst records from 140 districts of a fictional water utility, they test priors by simulation, fit and diagnose Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, pool small districts hierarchically, check and compare models by predictive accuracy, and verify code by simulation-based calibration. The last day turns posteriors into a replacement plan by expected utility and prices a proposed leak survey by its value of information.

Learning outcomes

By the end, the participant can:

  1. Applied

    Implement a Bayesian workflow from prior predictive simulation to sampler diagnosis.

    Given a burst-count model with network length as exposure.

    Evidence a notebook with prior draws, diagnostics and a reason for each prior.

  2. Performance

    Design a hierarchical model for districts with very unequal data and defend the pooling it implies.

    Given 140 districts, a third with fewer than five recorded bursts.

    Evidence no-, complete- and partial-pooling estimates with a written defence.

  3. Performance

    Evaluate competing models by posterior predictive checks and approximate leave-one-out cross-validation and choose one for the decision.

    Given three candidate models of the same data.

    Evidence a comparison table with check plots and the reasoning.

  4. Performance

    Diagnose an error in a model implementation by parameter recovery and simulation-based calibration.

    Given a model with a seeded coding error.

    Evidence calibration plots that locate the error and a corrected model that passes.

  5. Performance

    Decide a replacement plan by expected utility under the posterior and price further data by its value of information.

    Given costs, a capital budget and an offered acoustic leak survey.

    Evidence a two-page memo with the plan, its expected loss and a verdict on the survey.

  6. Performance

    Review another analyst's Bayesian analysis for priors, computation, checking and reporting.

    Given an analysis with six planted flaws.

    Evidence a review that finds at least four flaws and ranks them by effect on the decision.

Topics

Priors and computation

  • Generative models and weakly informative priors
  • Prior predictive simulation
  • Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, diagnostics and reparameterisation
  • When approximate inference suffices

Hierarchical models

  • Partial pooling and shrinkage
  • Group-level predictors
  • Under-reporting as measurement error

Checking and comparing

  • Posterior predictive checks
  • Approximate leave-one-out cross-validation
  • Simulation-based calibration
  • Prior sensitivity

Decisions

  • Expected utility over posterior draws
  • Value of information
  • Expected loss explained to risk owners

Day by day

Four sessions a day. Each session presents the concepts with a demonstration, then ends in a lab.

Tools and platforms

These are examples. Any equivalent tool may be used instead.

Probabilistic programming
Stan through CmdStanPy or CmdStanR, PyMC with Bambi, NumPyro, brms for R
Diagnostics and model comparison
ArviZ, loo and bayesplot for R
Analysis environment
Python through uv with Jupyter or marimo, R with Quarto

How the work is assessed

  • AI-off supervised check: read diagnostics, choose a pooling structure, compute an expected loss by hand (20%)
  • Capstone rubric: model and priors 20, computation 20, checking 25, decision 25, reproducibility 10 (60%)
  • Oral with a peer-model review (20%)
To pass
70% overall, with at least 60% on the capstone rubric and on the supervised check

Use of AI in the course

AI-off for supervised checks and the first model written each day. AI-tutor in other labs: explains diagnostics, writes no model code. AI-full in the capstone, defended orally.

AI-off: no AI assistance. AI-tutor: an assistant that hints and explains but does not give the answer. AI-full: any assistance; the participant owns the result and defends it.
